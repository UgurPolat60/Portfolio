01 — Display

A screen is a wall of shutters.

Nothing in the panel emits the picture. Behind it there is a sheet of white LEDs that is simply on. In front of that sit two polarising filters turned ninety degrees to each other — crossed like that, they block everything. Between them is the liquid crystal.

Liquid crystal is the trick. It is a fluid whose molecules line up, and lining them up twists the light passing through. Untwisted, the light arrives at the second filter in the wrong orientation and is stopped: black. Apply a voltage and the molecules rotate, the light twists with them, and some of it gets through. The voltage does not make light. It decides how much light is allowed past.

Every pixel is three of those shutters side by side with a red, a green and a blue filter on top. Your eye cannot resolve them individually, so it averages. That is the entire mechanism: a lamp, two filters, and two million controllable twists.

The panel is addressed one row at a time by a grid of transistors, one per subpixel — that is what the TFT in "TFT LCD" means. The controller walks down the rows writing voltages, and when it reaches the bottom it starts again. If it starts drawing a new image before the previous one has finished arriving, you see the seam. That is screen tearing, and it is why the refresh cycle and the thing generating frames have to agree.

02 — Under the deck

Everything is arranged around heat.

Lift the keyboard away and the layout stops looking arbitrary. The two chips that turn electricity into heat sit at the back, closest to the vents, with the cooling laid straight across them. The battery — physically the largest object in the machine — takes the front, where your wrists are, because it is the one component that must stay cool and must not be punctured.

Nothing here is placed for convenience. Memory goes where the traces to the processor can stay short and equal in length. The storage goes wherever is left. The design constraint is not space, it is watts: a desktop can throw a kilogram of metal at the problem, a laptop has twenty millimetres and your lap.

It is also why laptops are hard to repair. Every millimetre saved is a millimetre not spent on connectors, so parts get soldered down rather than socketed. The two memory modules you are about to see are, on many machines, the only thing left you are allowed to change.

03 — Processor

The CPU is a machine for doing one thing at a time, extremely quickly.

Underneath the metal lid is a piece of silicon the size of a fingernail with billions of transistors etched into it. A transistor is a switch with no moving parts. Wire enough of them together and you can build a circuit that adds two numbers; wire enough of those together and you can build something that reads an instruction and does what it says.

That is the whole loop, and it has not changed since the 1940s: fetch the next instruction from memory, decode what it means, execute it, write the result back. A modern core does this billions of times a second, and it does not do them one at a time — it runs them down a pipeline like an assembly line, so while one instruction is being executed the next is already being decoded and the one after that is being fetched.

Which creates the problem that defines modern processors. To keep the pipeline full, the chip has to know which instruction comes next — but if the code has just hit an if, that answer is not known yet. So the processor guesses. It predicts the branch, runs ahead on the assumption, and throws the work away if it guessed wrong. Good prediction is worth more than clock speed, and this is where a large share of the transistors go.

The other share goes to cache. Main memory is roughly a hundred times slower than the core, so a core that had to wait for it would spend almost all its life idle. The fix is a hierarchy of progressively larger, slower memories built onto the chip itself — a few kilobytes right next to the execution units, a few megabytes shared between cores. Almost all of what a processor does is arranging never to have to ask main memory anything.

And why several cores instead of one faster one? Because heat scales with the square of voltage, and voltage has to rise with frequency. Doubling the clock costs far more than double the power. Two cores at the same clock cost about twice — so the industry stopped making one core faster around 2005 and started making more of them, and handed the problem of using them to us.

04 — Memory

RAM is the desk. Storage is the filing cabinet.

A bit in memory is one capacitor and one transistor. The capacitor either holds a charge or does not, and the transistor decides whether you are allowed to touch it. Billions of those in a grid is what a memory module is.

Capacitors leak. Within milliseconds the charge has drained enough to be unreadable, so the chip continuously reads every row and writes it straight back, thousands of times a second, purely to remind itself what it was holding. That is what the D in DRAM means — dynamic — and it is why memory forgets everything the moment power is removed. Not a design failure: a capacitor that held its charge forever would take much longer to change.

Reading is not random either, despite the name. The chip opens an entire row into a buffer first, then reads columns out of it. Hitting the same open row again is fast; jumping to another row means closing the first one and paying the full latency. This is exactly why code that walks through memory in order is dramatically faster than code that jumps around, and why the layout of your data can matter more than the algorithm.

There are two modules here rather than one for a reason. Each has its own path to the processor, so both can be read at once — dual channel, roughly double the bandwidth for the same capacity. A machine with one module and a machine with two of half the size hold the same amount and do not perform the same.

05 — Graphics

The GPU is not a faster CPU. It is a wider one.

A processor core is built to finish one chain of dependent work as fast as possible. Everything in it — branch prediction, out-of-order execution, deep caches — exists to reduce the time between starting an instruction and finishing it. That is a latency machine.

A graphics chip is built on the opposite bet: that you have thousands of pieces of work that do not depend on each other. It has thousands of small arithmetic units, and rather than giving each its own control logic, it runs them in lockstep — one instruction issued to a whole group at once, each acting on its own data. Cheap per unit, and pointless unless the work really is that uniform.

Rendering is exactly that uniform. Two million pixels, and the colour of each is computed by running the same short program on different inputs. The chip does not need to finish any one pixel quickly; it needs to have thousands in flight. When one group stalls waiting for a texture, it swaps in another group and keeps the units busy. It hides latency instead of fighting it.

It has its own memory for the same reason, and that memory is optimised for bandwidth over latency — wide buses, many banks, moving enormous amounts of data with no concern for how long any individual access takes.

Which is why this same chip turned out to run neural networks. Training a model is a very large number of small multiply-accumulates with no dependencies between them — structurally the same shape as shading pixels. The hardware was not repurposed. The problem simply turned out to be the one it was already built for.

06 — Cooling

Heat is the real limit, not the silicon.

Every switch inside those chips leaks a little energy each time it flips, and they flip billions of times a second. All of it becomes heat, in a box you rest on your legs. Transistors also get leakier as they get hotter, so heat produces more heat, and past a certain point the chip cannot cool itself faster than it is warming.

Long before that, the firmware intervenes. It reads the die temperature thousands of times a second and simply lowers the clock — thermal throttling. This is why two laptops with identical specifications can perform completely differently, and why the interesting number is not the peak clock but the one it can hold for ten minutes.

The copper pipes are the clever part. Each is sealed, evacuated, and holds a small amount of water. At the hot end the low pressure lets that water boil at well under a hundred degrees, and boiling absorbs a great deal of energy. The vapour rushes to the cold end, condenses back to liquid on the cooler wall, releases that energy into the fins — and a wick lining the inside draws the liquid back to the hot end by capillary action, with no pump and no moving parts. A heatpipe moves heat many times better than a solid copper bar of the same size, because it is not conducting it. It is carrying it.

The fans are the least interesting component and the only one you hear. All they do is drag room-temperature air across those fins fast enough to take the heat away. The pipes have already done the difficult part.

07 — Mainboard

The board is the argument that these parts are one machine.

It looks like a green plate with components on it. It is closer to ten thin sheets of copper laminated together, most of them not carrying signals at all — they are solid planes of ground and power, there so that every signal has a clean path directly beneath it to return along. Get those planes wrong and the machine works on the bench and fails in the field.

At these speeds a copper track stops behaving like a wire and starts behaving like a pipe for a wave. If the width or the spacing to the plane below changes, part of the signal reflects back down the track and collides with what is still arriving. So the tracks are drawn to a controlled impedance, and where a group of them has to arrive together, they are drawn to the same length — which is what the little zig-zags on a memory bus are. They are not decoration. They are one track waiting for the others.

That physical limit is why buses went serial. Sending sixty-four bits side by side means sixty-four tracks that must arrive within picoseconds of each other, and that gets impossible as speeds rise. PCIe sends far fewer wires much faster, each as a differential pair — two tracks carrying opposite versions of the same signal, so any interference hits both equally and subtracting one from the other cancels it.

And the unglamorous part: the power stages. The battery supplies something near twelve volts; the processor wants about one, at very high current, and it wants it to stay there while its draw changes by a factor of ten in microseconds. The chokes and capacitors clustered around the socket are switching converters doing exactly that, millions of times a second. When a laptop dies young, this is usually what died.
